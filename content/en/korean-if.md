"If you have time", "if it rains", "if you don't know, ask": conditions come up in almost every conversation. In Korean you don't need a separate word for "if". You attach the ending **-(으)면** to the verb or adjective, and the whole clause becomes a condition.

> **Quick answer:** Drop **-다** and add **-면** if the stem ends in a vowel or ㄹ, or **-으면** if it ends in any other consonant: 가다 → **가면** (*gamyeon*, "if (I) go"), 먹다 → **먹으면** (*meogeumyeon*, "if (I) eat"), 만들다 → **만들면** (*mandeulmyeon*). The -(으)면 clause always comes first: **비가 오면 집에 있을 거예요** (*Biga omyeon jibe isseul geoyeyo*), "If it rains, I'll stay home." The same ending also means "when / whenever" for things that will happen or always happen. For wishes, use **-았/었으면 좋겠어요** ("I hope / I wish").

This lesson assumes you know the [present tense -아요/-어요](/learn-korean/en/korean-present-tense/) and the [future tense -(으)ㄹ 거예요](/learn-korean/en/korean-future-tense/). You have already seen -(으)면 in "must not" (-(으)면 안 돼요) in the lesson on [have to in Korean](/learn-korean/en/korean-have-to/). Romanization follows the Revised Romanization system.

## How to form -(으)면

The rule depends only on the last letter of the stem (the part before 다).

| Stem ends in | Add | Verb | -(으)면 form | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| a vowel | -면 | 가다 (to go) | 가면 | gamyeon | if (I) go |
| a vowel | -면 | 보다 (to see, watch) | 보면 | bomyeon | if (I) see |
| a vowel | -면 | 마시다 (to drink) | 마시면 | masimyeon | if (I) drink |
| a vowel | -면 | 하다 (to do) | 하면 | hamyeon | if (I) do |
| a vowel | -면 | 피곤하다 (to be tired) | 피곤하면 | pigonhamyeon | if (you're) tired |
| ㄹ | -면 | 만들다 (to make) | 만들면 | mandeulmyeon | if (I) make |
| ㄹ | -면 | 살다 (to live) | 살면 | salmyeon | if (I) live |
| ㄹ | -면 | 멀다 (to be far) | 멀면 | meolmyeon | if (it's) far |
| a consonant | -으면 | 먹다 (to eat) | 먹으면 | meogeumyeon | if (I) eat |
| a consonant | -으면 | 읽다 (to read) | 읽으면 | ilgeumyeon | if (I) read |
| a consonant | -으면 | 있다 (to have, be there) | 있으면 | isseumyeon | if (there) is / if (I) have |
| a consonant | -으면 | 없다 (to not have) | 없으면 | eopseumyeon | if (there) isn't |
| a consonant | -으면 | 좋다 (to be good) | 좋으면 | joeumyeon | if (it's) good |

Stems ending in ㄹ are the one trap: they behave like vowel stems, so it's **만들면**, never 만들으면.

### Pronunciation tip

When -으면 follows a consonant, that consonant slides into the next syllable: 먹으면 is pronounced [머그면], 있으면 [이쓰면] and 없으면 [업쓰면]. The ㅎ in 좋다 goes silent: 좋으면 sounds like [조으면]. The romanization above already reflects this.

## Irregular verbs

A few verb groups change their stem before -으면. These are the same irregulars you meet with -아요/-어요.

| Type | Verb | -(으)면 form | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ㄷ irregular | 듣다 (to listen) | 들으면 | deureumyeon | if (I) listen |
| ㄷ irregular | 걷다 (to walk) | 걸으면 | georeumyeon | if (I) walk |
| ㅂ irregular | 춥다 (to be cold) | 추우면 | chuumyeon | if (it's) cold |
| ㅂ irregular | 덥다 (to be hot) | 더우면 | deoumyeon | if (it's) hot |
| ㅂ irregular | 맵다 (to be spicy) | 매우면 | maeumyeon | if (it's) spicy |
| ㅂ irregular | 어렵다 (to be difficult) | 어려우면 | eoryeoumyeon | if (it's) difficult |
| ㅅ irregular | 낫다 (to get better) | 나으면 | naeumyeon | if (it) gets better |
| ㅎ irregular | 그렇다 (to be so) | 그러면 | geureomyeon | if so, then |

르 verbs such as 모르다 ("to not know") end in a vowel, so they simply take -면: **모르면** (*moreumyeon*).

## Nouns: -(이)면

To say "if it's a ___", add **이면** after a consonant and **면** (or 이면) after a vowel.

| Noun | Form | Romanization | Example meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 학생 (student) | 학생이면 | haksaengimyeon | if (you're) a student |
| 친구 (friend) | 친구면 | chingumyeon | if (it's) a friend |
| 주말 (weekend) | 주말이면 | jumarimyeon | if it's the weekend |

**학생이면 할인돼요** (*Haksaengimyeon harindwaeyo*), "If you're a student, you get a discount."

## Using -(으)면 in sentences

The condition comes first and the result comes second. Only the final verb shows tense and politeness.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 시간이 있으면 같이 영화 볼까요? | Sigani isseumyeon gachi yeonghwa bolkkayo? | If you have time, shall we watch a movie together? |
| 비가 오면 집에 있을 거예요. | Biga omyeon jibe isseul geoyeyo. | If it rains, I'll stay home. |
| 배고프면 이거 드세요. | Baegopeumyeon igeo deuseyo. | If you're hungry, have this. |
| 피곤하면 좀 쉬세요. | Pigonhamyeon jom swiseyo. | If you're tired, rest a bit. |
| 모르면 물어보세요. | Moreumyeon mureoboseyo. | If you don't know, ask. |
| 매우면 물 마셔요. | Maeumyeon mul masyeoyo. | If it's spicy, drink some water. |
| 싫으면 안 해도 돼요. | Sireumyeon an haedo dwaeyo. | If you don't want to, you don't have to. |
| 돈이 없으면 못 가요. | Doni eopseumyeon mot gayo. | If I don't have money, I can't go. |

For "shall we?" see [-(으)ㄹ까요](/learn-korean/en/korean-shall-we-eulkkayo/). To make the condition negative, use 안 or -지 않으면 as in [Korean negation](/learn-korean/en/korean-negation/): **안 오면** ("if (he) doesn't come"), **바쁘지 않으면** ("if you're not busy").

### Adding 만약 for "if"

**만약(에)** (*manyak*) is like putting extra stress on "if" or "suppose". It's optional, but it makes a hypothetical clear from the start of the sentence.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 만약 내일 비가 오면 어떻게 해요? | Manyak naeil biga omyeon eotteoke haeyo? | If it rains tomorrow, what do we do? |
| 만약 길을 잃으면 저한테 전화하세요. | Manyak gireul ireumyeon jeohante jeonhwahaseyo. | If you get lost, call me. |

## -(으)면 as "when" and "whenever"

Korean uses the same ending for "when" if the event is in the future or happens regularly.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 집에 가면 전화해 주세요. | Jibe gamyeon jeonhwahae juseyo. | Call me when you get home. |
| 봄이 되면 꽃이 피어요. | Bomi doemyeon kkochi pieoyo. | When spring comes, flowers bloom. |
| 이 노래를 들으면 기분이 좋아져요. | I noraereul deureumyeon gibuni joajyeoyo. | Whenever I hear this song, I feel better. |
| 한국에 가면 한국어로 주문하고 싶어요. | Hanguge gamyeon hangugeoro jumunhago sipeoyo. | When I go to Korea, I want to order in Korean. |

The last example uses [-고 싶어요](/learn-korean/en/korean-want-to/), and 주세요 comes from the lesson on [please in Korean](/learn-korean/en/korean-please/).

## Don't put the future inside the -(으)면 clause

In English we say "if it rains", not "if it will rain". Korean is the same: the -(으)면 clause stays in its basic form, and the future goes on the final verb.

| Wrong | Right | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 내일 비가 올 거면 집에 있을 거예요. | 내일 비가 오면 집에 있을 거예요. | If it rains tomorrow, I'll stay home. |
| 한국에 갈 거면 연락할게요. | 한국에 가면 연락할게요. | I'll get in touch when I go to Korea. |

## -(으)면 vs -(으)ㄹ 때

Both can translate as "when", but they are not the same. **-(으)면** is a condition (if/whenever this happens, then that). **-(으)ㄹ 때** points to a moment in time, and it's the one you need for a single event in the past.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 한국에 가면 떡볶이를 먹을 거예요. | Hanguge gamyeon tteokbokkireul meogeul geoyeyo. | When I go to Korea, I'll eat tteokbokki. |
| 한국에 갔을 때 떡볶이를 먹었어요. | Hanguge gasseul ttae tteokbokkireul meogeosseoyo. | When I went to Korea, I ate tteokbokki. |
| 한국에 가면 항상 떡볶이를 먹었어요. | Hanguge gamyeon hangsang tteokbokkireul meogeosseoyo. | Whenever I went to Korea, I always ate tteokbokki. |

So -(으)면 can sit before a past verb only when it describes a habit ("whenever"), not a one-time event.

## Three very useful patterns

### -(으)면 돼요: "you just need to"

Literally "if you do it, it works". It's how Koreans tell you the simple way to do something, and it's everywhere when you ask for directions or instructions.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 여기에 이름을 쓰면 돼요. | Yeogie ireumeul sseumyeon dwaeyo. | You just write your name here. |
| 2호선을 타면 돼요. | Ihoseoneul tamyeon dwaeyo. | You can just take Line 2. |
| 어떻게 하면 돼요? | Eotteoke hamyeon dwaeyo? | What should I do? / How do I do it? |

### -(으)면 안 돼요: "you must not"

The opposite: "if you do it, it's not okay". **여기서 사진을 찍으면 안 돼요** (*Yeogiseo sajineul jjigeumyeon an dwaeyo*), "You can't take photos here." See [have to in Korean](/learn-korean/en/korean-have-to/) for more.

### -았/었으면 좋겠어요: "I hope / I wish"

Add the past marker -았/었 before -으면, then **좋겠어요** ("it would be good"). Even though it uses a past form, it talks about hopes for now or the future.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 주말에 날씨가 좋았으면 좋겠어요. | Jumare nalssiga joasseumyeon jokesseoyo. | I hope the weather is nice this weekend. |
| 시간이 더 있었으면 좋겠어요. | Sigani deo isseosseumyeon jokesseoyo. | I wish I had more time. |
| 빨리 방학이 왔으면 좋겠어요. | Ppalli banghagi wasseumyeon jokesseoyo. | I wish the school break would come soon. |

The present form, **-(으)면 좋겠어요** (같이 가면 좋겠어요, "It would be nice to go together"), is also correct; the -았/었으면 version sounds more like a heartfelt wish. Form -았/었 the same way as in the [past tense](/learn-korean/en/korean-past-tense/): 가다 → 갔으면, 먹다 → 먹었으면, 하다 → 했으면.

## Politeness and honorifics

-(으)면 itself has no politeness level; the final verb carries it. Compare 시간 있으면 와 (casual), 시간 있으면 와요 (polite) and 시간 있으면 오세요 (polite and respectful). When the person in the condition deserves respect, add **-시** before -면: 가시면, 오시면, 있으시면. You'll hear this constantly in shops and offices:

**궁금한 점이 있으시면 말씀해 주세요.** (*Gunggeumhan jeomi isseusimyeon malsseumhae juseyo.*) "If you have any questions, please let us know."

For more on choosing the right level, see [formal vs informal Korean](/learn-korean/en/korean-speech-levels/).

## Bonus: 그러면 and 그럼

**그러면** (*geureomyeon*) is 그렇다 + -면, "if so", and it works as "then / in that case" at the start of a sentence. In speech it is usually shortened to **그럼** (*geureom*): **그럼 내일 봐요** (*Geureom naeil bwayo*), "See you tomorrow, then."

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 먹면 | 먹으면 | consonant stems need -으면 |
| 만들으면 | 만들면 | ㄹ stems take -면 like vowel stems |
| 듣으면 | 들으면 | ㄷ irregular: ㄷ becomes ㄹ before 으 |
| 춥으면 | 추우면 | ㅂ irregular: ㅂ becomes 우 |
| 비가 올 거면 | 비가 오면 | no future inside the -(으)면 clause |
| 어제 한국에 가면 | 어제 한국에 갔을 때 | one-time past events use -(으)ㄹ 때 |
| 가 면 | 가면 | -면 is attached with no space |

## FAQ

**Is there a separate word for "if" in Korean?** Not really. The meaning comes from the ending -(으)면. **만약(에)** can be added at the start for emphasis, but the sentence still needs -(으)면 (or -다면): 만약 비가 오면, never just 만약 비가 와요.

**How do I say "If I were you" in Korean?** Use **-(이)라면**, the more hypothetical form of -(이)면: **내가 너라면 안 갈 거야** (*Naega neoramyeon an gal geoya*), "If I were you, I wouldn't go" (casual). The -다면 family (간다면, 많다면, -(이)라면) sounds more imaginary than -(으)면 and is a good next step once -(으)면 feels natural.

**Why is it 있으면 and not 있면?** 있다 ends in the consonant ㅆ, so it takes -으면. The same goes for 없으면, 괜찮으면 and 좋으면.

**Can -(으)면 come at the end of a sentence?** In casual speech, yes, as a soft wish or suggestion: **같이 가면...** ("It'd be nice if we went together..."). In polite speech, finish the thought with 좋겠어요: 같이 가면 좋겠어요.

**What's the difference between -(으)면 and -거든?** Both can mean "if". -거든 is used only when the second part is a request, suggestion or plan (도착하거든 연락해), and it's less common in everyday speech for learners. -(으)면 works in every case, so start with it.

**What's next?** -(으)면 pairs naturally with [can and can't](/learn-korean/en/korean-can-cant/) (시간이 있으면 갈 수 있어요, "If I have time, I can go") and with [because](/learn-korean/en/korean-because/) when you explain your reasons.
