Good news first: Korean has one of the easiest writing systems in the world to learn. Hangul (한글) was designed on purpose, in the 15th century under King Sejong, so that ordinary people could learn to read quickly. Most learners can sound out simple words after an hour or two of focused practice.

> **Quick answer:** Hangul has **14 basic consonants** and **10 basic vowels**. Letters are grouped into square **syllable blocks**: a consonant, then a vowel, and sometimes a final consonant underneath. Learn the basic letters, learn how blocks are built, and you can read any Korean word aloud, even before you know what it means.

## Step 1: The 10 basic vowels

Vowels are built from three simple shapes: a dot or short stroke (originally the sky), a horizontal line (the earth) and a vertical line (a person). Here they are with the Revised Romanization used on Korean road signs.

| Letter | Romanization | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| ㅏ | a | "a" in *father* |
| ㅓ | eo | "u" in *cup*, or "o" in British *hot* |
| ㅗ | o | "o" in *go*, but short, without the "w" glide |
| ㅜ | u | "oo" in *food* |
| ㅡ | eu | like "oo" but with lips spread flat, as if smiling |
| ㅣ | i | "ee" in *see* |
| ㅑ | ya | "ya" in *yacht* |
| ㅕ | yeo | "yu" in *yummy* |
| ㅛ | yo | "yo" in *yoga* |
| ㅠ | yu | "you" |

Notice the pattern: adding a second short stroke adds a "y" sound. ㅏ (a) becomes ㅑ (ya), ㅗ (o) becomes ㅛ (yo).

**Tip for English speakers:** ㅓ (eo) and ㅗ (o) are the pair people mix up most. ㅓ is open and relaxed, like a surprised "uh". ㅗ has rounded lips.

## Step 2: The 14 basic consonants

Many consonant shapes are pictures of where your mouth makes the sound. ㄱ shows the back of the tongue blocking the throat, ㄴ the tongue tip touching behind the teeth, ㅁ the closed mouth, ㅅ a tooth, and ㅇ the open throat.

| Letter | Romanization | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ㄱ | g / k | softer than English "g"; sounds like "k" at the end of a syllable |
| ㄴ | n | |
| ㄷ | d / t | |
| ㄹ | r / l | a quick tap like the "tt" in American *butter* between vowels; "l" at the end |
| ㅁ | m | |
| ㅂ | b / p | |
| ㅅ | s | before ㅣ it sounds like "sh" (시 = "shi") |
| ㅇ | silent / ng | silent at the start of a block, "ng" at the end |
| ㅈ | j | |
| ㅊ | ch | with a puff of air |
| ㅋ | k | with a puff of air |
| ㅌ | t | with a puff of air |
| ㅍ | p | with a puff of air |
| ㅎ | h | |

Look at the families: ㄱ → ㅋ, ㄷ → ㅌ, ㅂ → ㅍ, ㅈ → ㅊ. Adding a stroke adds a puff of air (aspiration). Hold a tissue in front of your mouth: it should move for ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ and ㅊ, and barely move for ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ and ㅈ.

## Step 3: Build syllable blocks

Korean is never written one letter after another like English. Letters are packed into blocks, one block per syllable. Every block starts with a consonant and contains a vowel.

- **Tall vowels** (ㅏ ㅓ ㅣ ㅑ ㅕ) go to the **right** of the consonant: ㄴ + ㅏ = **나** (na)
- **Flat vowels** (ㅗ ㅜ ㅡ ㅛ ㅠ) go **under** the consonant: ㄴ + ㅗ = **노** (no)
- A syllable that starts with a vowel sound uses the silent ㅇ as a placeholder: ㅇ + ㅏ = **아** (a)

Try reading these: 가 (ga), 고 (go), 마 (ma), 무 (mu), 하 (ha), 이 (i), 우 (u).

## Step 4: Final consonants (받침, batchim)

A third letter can sit at the bottom of a block. This final consonant is called *batchim*.

ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ = **한** (han) · ㄱ + ㅜ + ㄱ = **국** (guk) → **한국** (Hanguk, "Korea")

Even though many letters can appear at the bottom, they collapse into only **seven sounds** at the end of a syllable: k, n, t, l, m, p and ng. For example, ㅅ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ and ㅎ at the bottom are all pronounced as a clipped "t". That's why 옷 (clothes) sounds like "ot".

## Step 5: Practice with real words

Cover the right column and try to read each word aloud.

| Word | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 바나나 | ba-na-na | banana |
| 커피 | keo-pi | coffee |
| 라면 | ra-myeon | ramen noodles |
| 김치 | gim-chi | kimchi |
| 서울 | seo-ul | Seoul |
| 사랑 | sa-rang | love |
| 친구 | chin-gu | friend |
| 감사합니다 | gam-sa-ham-ni-da | thank you |

Notice 감사합니다 is pronounced "gam-sa-**ham**-ni-da", not "hab-ni-da". Sounds change slightly when certain letters meet. You don't need these rules on day one; you'll pick them up by listening.

## The remaining letters (for later)

Modern Hangul also has five **double consonants**, said with a tense throat and no puff of air: ㄲ (kk), ㄸ (tt), ㅃ (pp), ㅆ (ss), ㅉ (jj). There are also combined vowels such as ㅐ (ae), ㅔ (e), ㅘ (wa), ㅝ (wo), ㅚ (oe), ㅟ (wi) and ㅢ (ui). In everyday speech ㅐ and ㅔ sound almost the same, like the "e" in *bed*.

## A one-hour study plan

1. **10 min:** Learn the 10 basic vowels. Write each one five times while saying it aloud.
2. **15 min:** Learn the 14 consonants by family (ㄱ ㅋ, ㄷ ㅌ, ㅂ ㅍ, ㅈ ㅊ).
3. **10 min:** Build blocks: pair every consonant with ㅏ, then with ㅗ.
4. **10 min:** Add final consonants and read 한국, 사랑, 친구.
5. **15 min:** Read real Korean around you: K-pop song titles, menu items, or the word list above.

## FAQ

**Should I rely on romanization?** Use it only for the first day or two. Romanization can't show Korean sounds accurately (ㅓ and ㅗ both look like an "o" to English speakers), and reading Hangul directly is faster within a week.

**What's the best order to learn Korean after Hangul?** Basic greetings and numbers, then simple sentence patterns like "N이에요/예요" (it is N). We'll cover these in the next lessons.

**Is handwriting important?** Not at first. Write each letter enough to remember its shape, then spend most of your time reading and listening.
