Particles are the small syllables glued to the end of Korean nouns. They tell you what each word is doing in the sentence, and once you understand the first three, you can build and read real Korean sentences.

> **Quick answer:** Korean uses particles after nouns instead of word order to show grammar. **은/는 (eun/neun)** marks the **topic** ("as for…"), **이/가 (i/ga)** marks the **subject**, and **을/를 (eul/reul)** marks the **object**. Use the first form (은, 이, 을) after a noun ending in a **consonant** and the second (는, 가, 를) after a **vowel**: 책**은**, 커피**는**. In **저는 커피를 마셔요** (*jeoneun keopireul masyeoyo*), "I drink coffee", 저 (I) is the topic and 커피 (coffee) is the object.

If you can't read Hangul yet, start with [Learn Hangul in One Hour](/learn-korean/en/hangul-alphabet/). The example sentences use the polite **-요** ending explained in [basic Korean greetings](/learn-korean/en/korean-greetings/). Romanization follows the Revised Romanization system.

## Why Korean needs particles

English relies on word order: "The dog bites the man" and "The man bites the dog" mean different things. Korean puts the verb at the end (subject, object, verb), and the particles do the job word order does in English.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 저는 커피를 마셔요. | Jeoneun keopireul masyeoyo. | I drink coffee. |
| 커피를 저는 마셔요. | Keopireul jeoneun masyeoyo. | I drink coffee. (stress on "coffee") |
| 민수가 김치를 먹어요. | Minsuga gimchireul meogeoyo. | Minsu eats kimchi. |

The first two sentences mean the same thing because the particles stay attached to their nouns. You can move words around a little and the meaning holds, though the verb always stays at the end.

## Choosing the right form: consonant or vowel?

Each of the three core particles has two shapes. The choice depends only on the last sound of the noun: does the final syllable have a bottom consonant (받침, *batchim*) or not?

| Job | After a consonant | After a vowel |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | 은 (eun) | 는 (neun) |
| Subject | 이 (i) | 가 (ga) |
| Object | 을 (eul) | 를 (reul) |

Here are the same particles on a word that ends in a consonant (책, *chaek*, book) and one that ends in a vowel (커피, *keopi*, coffee):

| Particle | 책 (book) | 커피 (coffee) |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | 책은 (chaegeun) | 커피는 (keopineun) |
| Subject | 책이 (chaegi) | 커피가 (keopiga) |
| Object | 책을 (chaegeul) | 커피를 (keopireul) |

Notice how the final consonant of 책 links to the particle's vowel: 책을 sounds like *chae-geul*, not *chaek-eul*. This linking happens with every consonant-ending noun, so 물을 (water, object) is *mu-reul*, and 이름이 (name, subject) is *i-reu-mi*.

### Three irregular forms you'll use daily

When 가 attaches to these pronouns, the pronoun changes shape:

| Pronoun | + 가 | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 저 (I, polite) | 제가 | jega | I (as subject) |
| 나 (I, casual) | 내가 | naega | I (as subject) |
| 누구 (who) | 누가 | nuga | who (as subject) |

So you say **제가 할게요** (*jega halgeyo*), "I'll do it", never 저가. And "Who came?" is **누가 왔어요?** (*nuga wasseoyo?*), never 누구가.

## 을/를: the object marker

This one is the easiest. 을/를 marks the thing that receives the action: what you eat, drink, watch, study or buy.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 한국어를 공부해요. | Hangugeoreul gongbuhaeyo. | I study Korean. |
| 물을 마셔요. | Mureul masyeoyo. | I drink water. |
| 드라마를 봐요. | Deuramareul bwayo. | I watch dramas. |
| 표를 샀어요. | Pyoreul sasseoyo. | I bought a ticket. |

Korean often drops the subject when it's obvious, which is why these sentences don't need 저는.

## 은/는 vs 이/가: the big question

This is the most-asked grammar question from learners of Korean, and it doesn't map neatly onto English. Both often translate as the subject. The difference is about **focus and information**. These rules cover the vast majority of everyday sentences.

### 1. Use 은/는 to say what you're talking about

은/는 sets the topic, like "as for…". It's what you use for introductions and general statements.

- **저는 학생이에요.** (*Jeoneun haksaengieyo.*) "I'm a student." (As for me, I'm a student.)
- **한국은 겨울이 추워요.** (*Hangugeun gyeouri chuwoyo.*) "Winters in Korea are cold."

### 2. Use 은/는 for contrast

When you compare two things, 은/는 highlights the contrast, like stressing a word in English.

- **커피는 좋아해요. 그런데 차는 안 좋아해요.** (*Keopineun joahaeyo. Geureonde chaneun an joahaeyo.*) "Coffee, I like. But tea, I don't."

### 3. Use 이/가 for new information, 은/는 once it's known

When something enters the conversation for the first time, it usually takes 이/가. Once everyone knows what you're talking about, it switches to 은/는. Korean folk tales show this perfectly:

- **옛날에 한 남자가 살았어요. 그 남자는 아주 가난했어요.** (*Yennare han namjaga sarasseoyo. Geu namjaneun aju ganhaesseoyo.*) "Once upon a time, a man lived. The man was very poor."

English does something similar with "a man" and "the man".

### 4. Use 이/가 to answer "who?" or "what?"

When the subject is the answer, the new piece of information, it takes 이/가. Question words like 누가 (who) and 뭐가 (what) always take 이/가, and so does the answer.

- **누가 왔어요?** (*Nuga wasseoyo?*) "Who came?"
- **민수가 왔어요.** (*Minsuga wasseoyo.*) "Minsu came."

Answering 민수는 왔어요 would sound like "Minsu came (but others didn't)", which is contrast, not an answer.

### 5. Use 이/가 with certain verbs and adjectives

Some expressions almost always take 이/가 on the thing being described. Learn them as set phrases:

| Pattern | Example | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ___이/가 있어요 / 없어요 | 시간이 없어요. | Sigani eopseoyo. | I don't have time. |
| ___이/가 아파요 | 머리가 아파요. | Meoriga apayo. | My head hurts. |
| ___이/가 좋아요 | 이 노래가 좋아요. | I noraega joayo. | I like this song. |
| ___이/가 아니에요 | 학생이 아니에요. | Haksaengi anieyo. | I'm not a student. |
| ___이/가 고파요 | 배가 고파요. | Baega gopayo. | I'm hungry. |
| ___이/가 뭐예요? | 이름이 뭐예요? | Ireumi mwoyeyo? | What's your name? |

You'll also hear 이/가 when describing something you see right now: **날씨가 좋아요** (*nalssiga joayo*), "The weather is nice."

### 6. Both in one sentence

Korean happily uses both particles in the same sentence. 은/는 sets the big topic, and 이/가 marks the specific subject inside it.

- **저는 머리가 아파요.** (*Jeoneun meoriga apayo.*) "I have a headache." (As for me, my head hurts.)
- **토끼는 귀가 길어요.** (*Tokkineun gwiga gireoyo.*) "Rabbits have long ears." (As for rabbits, the ears are long.)

### 7. Inside a describing clause, use 이/가

When a subject sits inside a clause that describes a noun, it takes 이/가, not 은/는.

- **제가 만든 김치예요.** (*Jega mandeun gimchiyeyo.*) "This is kimchi I made."

### Cheat sheet

| Situation | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Introducing yourself | 은/는 | 저는 마이클이에요. |
| General statement or fact | 은/는 | 한국은 겨울이 추워요. |
| Comparing or contrasting | 은/는 | 커피는 좋아해요. |
| First mention in a story | 이/가 | 한 남자가 살았어요. |
| Answering "who" or "what" | 이/가 | 민수가 왔어요. |
| With 있어요, 없어요, 아파요, 좋아요 | 이/가 | 시간이 없어요. |
| Describing what you see now | 이/가 | 날씨가 좋아요. |

## Dropping particles in conversation

In casual speech and text messages, Koreans often leave particles out when the meaning is clear: **커피 마실래요?** (*Keopi masillaeyo?*), "Do you want coffee?" or **이거 뭐예요?** (*Igeo mwoyeyo?*), "What's this?" The object marker 을/를 is the one most often dropped.

As a learner, keep your particles in. It's always correct, it's expected in writing, and dropping them is something you'll pick up naturally by listening.

## Six more particles to know next

The three core particles get you started. These six come up in almost every conversation, and each will get its own lesson.

| Particle | Meaning | Example | Romanization | Translation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 에 (e) | to, at (a place or a time) | 학교에 가요. | Hakgyoe gayo. | I go to school. |
| 에서 (eseo) | at, in (where an action happens) | 카페에서 공부해요. | Kapeeseo gongbuhaeyo. | I study at a café. |
| 도 (do) | also, too | 저도 좋아요. | Jeodo joayo. | I like it too. |
| 의 (ui) | 's, of (possession) | 친구의 책 | chinguui chaek | my friend's book |
| 하고 (hago) | and, with | 빵하고 우유 | ppanghago uyu | bread and milk |
| (으)로 (euro / ro) | by, toward (means or direction) | 지하철로 가요. | Jihacheollo gayo. | I go by subway. |

Two quick notes. **도 replaces 은/는, 이/가 and 을/를** instead of being added to them: 저도, not 저는도. And the possessive **의** is usually pronounced like 에 (*e*) in everyday speech; with pronouns it shortens, so 저의 becomes **제** (*je*, my).

You already met **에** with time in [Korean numbers](/learn-korean/en/korean-numbers/): 세 시에 만나요 (*se sie mannayo*), "Let's meet at three."

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 저가 | 제가 | 저 changes to 제 before 가 |
| 누구가 왔어요? | 누가 왔어요? | 누구 changes to 누 before 가 |
| 책를 | 책을 | 책 ends in a consonant |
| 커피이 | 커피가 | 커피 ends in a vowel |
| 이 노래를 좋아요. | 이 노래가 좋아요. / 이 노래를 좋아해요. | 좋아요 takes 이/가, 좋아해요 takes 을/를 |
| (Q: 누가 했어요?) 저는 했어요. | 제가 했어요. | Answers to "who" take 이/가 |

The 좋아요 vs 좋아해요 pair is worth memorizing: **이 노래가 좋아요** (*i noraega joayo*) and **이 노래를 좋아해요** (*i noraereul joahaeyo*) both mean "I like this song", but they take different particles.

## FAQ

**Is 은/는 the subject marker?** Not exactly. It marks the topic, which is often the subject, but it can also be an object or a time: **오늘은 바빠요** (*oneureun bappayo*), "Today, I'm busy." That's why textbooks call 은/는 the topic marker and 이/가 the subject marker.

**Will people understand me if I mix up 은/는 and 이/가?** Almost always, yes. The mix-up makes you sound a little off, not unclear. Getting the consonant/vowel form right (책을, not 책를) matters more for sounding natural at the beginning.

**Why is it 학생이에요 but 의사예요?** That's the same consonant/vowel idea, applied to the verb "to be". After a consonant use **이에요** (학생이에요, *haksaengieyo*, "I'm a student"), after a vowel use **예요** (의사예요, *uisayeyo*, "I'm a doctor").

**Do I need particles after names?** Yes, the same rules apply. A name ending in a consonant, like 지민 (Jimin), takes 이, 은 and 을: 지민이, 지민은, 지민을.

**How long does it take to master 은/는 vs 이/가?** The basic rules above take an afternoon. Feeling the difference instinctively comes with listening, so pay attention to particles whenever you watch a K-drama or read a webtoon.

**What should I learn next?** Review [Korean numbers](/learn-korean/en/korean-numbers/) and practice combining them with particles: **사과 세 개를 샀어요** (*sagwa se gaereul sasseoyo*), "I bought three apples."
