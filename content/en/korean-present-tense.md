Once you know a handful of verbs and the polite present tense, you can say what you do, what you like and where you're going. This one pattern unlocks more everyday Korean than any other grammar point.

> **Quick answer:** Take the dictionary form, drop **-다** to get the stem, then look at the stem's last vowel. If it's **ㅏ or ㅗ**, add **-아요** (*-ayo*): 살다 → **살아요**. With any other vowel, add **-어요** (*-eoyo*): 먹다 → **먹어요**. Every verb ending in **하다** becomes **해요** (*haeyo*): 공부하다 → **공부해요**. When the stem ends in a vowel, the two vowels usually merge: 가다 → **가요**, 보다 → **봐요**, 마시다 → **마셔요**.

The -요 ending is the polite style you already met in [basic Korean greetings](/learn-korean/en/korean-greetings/). It's the safe default with strangers, shop staff and anyone you don't know well. The examples use the particles from [Korean particles 은/는, 이/가, 을/를](/learn-korean/en/korean-particles/), and romanization follows the Revised Romanization system.

## Step 1: find the verb stem

Every Korean verb in the dictionary ends in **-다** (*-da*). Remove it and what's left is the stem, the part you conjugate.

| Dictionary form | Romanization | Stem | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 가다 | gada | 가- | to go |
| 먹다 | meokda | 먹- | to eat |
| 보다 | boda | 보- | to see, watch |
| 마시다 | masida | 마시- | to drink |
| 공부하다 | gongbuhada | 공부하- | to study |

## Step 2: choose -아요, -어요 or 해요

Look only at the **last vowel** of the stem. This is the same idea as choosing 은 or 는 in the particles lesson: one small check decides the ending.

| Last vowel of the stem | Ending | Example | Present tense |
|---|---|---|---|
| ㅏ or ㅗ | -아요 (ayo) | 살다 (to live) | 살아요 (sarayo) |
| anything else | -어요 (eoyo) | 먹다 (to eat) | 먹어요 (meogeoyo) |
| 하다 verbs | 해요 (haeyo) | 공부하다 (to study) | 공부해요 (gongbuhaeyo) |

### Stems ending in a consonant

When the stem ends in a consonant, you simply attach the ending. Nothing merges, and the consonant links to the next vowel when you pronounce it, so 먹어요 sounds like *meo-geo-yo*.

| Verb | Meaning | Present tense | Romanization |
|---|---|---|---|
| 앉다 | to sit | 앉아요 | anjayo |
| 받다 | to receive | 받아요 | badayo |
| 좋다 | to be good | 좋아요 | joayo |
| 놀다 | to play, hang out | 놀아요 | norayo |
| 먹다 | to eat | 먹어요 | meogeoyo |
| 읽다 | to read | 읽어요 | ilgeoyo |
| 있다 | to have, there is | 있어요 | isseoyo |
| 없다 | to not have, there isn't | 없어요 | eopseoyo |
| 웃다 | to laugh, smile | 웃어요 | useoyo |

### 하다 verbs: the biggest group

Hundreds of verbs are a noun plus 하다, and they all end in 해요. Learn this one and you've learned a large share of everyday verbs at once.

| Verb | Meaning | Present tense | Romanization |
|---|---|---|---|
| 하다 | to do | 해요 | haeyo |
| 공부하다 | to study | 공부해요 | gongbuhaeyo |
| 일하다 | to work | 일해요 | ilhaeyo |
| 좋아하다 | to like | 좋아해요 | joahaeyo |
| 운동하다 | to exercise | 운동해요 | undonghaeyo |
| 요리하다 | to cook | 요리해요 | yorihaeyo |

## Step 3: merge the vowels

When the stem ends in a vowel, Korean doesn't say two vowels in a row if it can avoid it. The ending blends into the stem. These contractions are not optional in normal speech: say 가요, never 가아요.

| Stem vowel + ending | Result | Example | Present tense | Romanization |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ㅏ + 아요 | ㅏ요 | 가다 (to go) | 가요 | gayo |
| ㅓ + 어요 | ㅓ요 | 서다 (to stand) | 서요 | seoyo |
| ㅗ + 아요 | ㅘ요 | 보다 (to see) | 봐요 | bwayo |
| ㅜ + 어요 | ㅝ요 | 배우다 (to learn) | 배워요 | baewoyo |
| ㅣ + 어요 | ㅕ요 | 마시다 (to drink) | 마셔요 | masyeoyo |
| ㅐ + 어요 | ㅐ요 | 보내다 (to send) | 보내요 | bonaeyo |
| ㅚ + 어요 | ㅙ요 | 되다 (to become) | 돼요 | dwaeyo |

A few more you'll use every day: 만나다 → **만나요** (*mannayo*, meet), 자다 → **자요** (*jayo*, sleep), 사다 → **사요** (*sayo*, buy), 오다 → **와요** (*wayo*, come), 주다 → **줘요** (*jwoyo*, give), 기다리다 → **기다려요** (*gidaryeoyo*, wait).

One common exception to the merging: 쉬다 (to rest) stays **쉬어요** (*swieoyo*), because ㅟ and ㅓ don't combine.

### Stems ending in ㅡ

When the stem ends in ㅡ, the ㅡ disappears. Then look at the vowel before it to pick ㅏ or ㅓ. If there's no vowel before it (a one-syllable stem), use ㅓ.

| Verb | Meaning | Present tense | Romanization | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 쓰다 | to write, use | 써요 | sseoyo | one syllable, so ㅓ |
| 크다 | to be big | 커요 | keoyo | one syllable, so ㅓ |
| 바쁘다 | to be busy | 바빠요 | bappayo | 바 has ㅏ |
| 아프다 | to hurt, be sick | 아파요 | apayo | 아 has ㅏ |
| 예쁘다 | to be pretty | 예뻐요 | yeppeoyo | 예 isn't ㅏ or ㅗ |

## Irregular verbs

A few groups change their final consonant before -아요/-어요. They follow patterns, so learn the pattern rather than each verb.

| Type | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ㅂ irregular | ㅂ becomes 우, then 어요 merges to 워요 | 덥다 → 더워요 (deowoyo, hot), 춥다 → 추워요 (chuwoyo, cold), 맵다 → 매워요 (maewoyo, spicy), 어렵다 → 어려워요 (eoryeowoyo, difficult), 쉽다 → 쉬워요 (swiwoyo, easy) |
| ㄷ irregular | ㄷ becomes ㄹ | 듣다 → 들어요 (deureoyo, listen), 걷다 → 걸어요 (georeoyo, walk), 묻다 → 물어요 (mureoyo, ask) |
| 르 irregular | 르 becomes ㄹ라요 or ㄹ러요 | 모르다 → 몰라요 (mollayo, not know), 빠르다 → 빨라요 (ppallayo, fast), 부르다 → 불러요 (bulleoyo, sing, call), 다르다 → 달라요 (dallayo, different) |
| ㅅ irregular | ㅅ drops, no merging | 낫다 → 나아요 (naayo, get better), 짓다 → 지어요 (jieoyo, build) |

Two cautions. **돕다** (to help) is ㅂ irregular but becomes **도와요** (*dowayo*), not 도워요. And not every verb with these consonants is irregular: 입다 → **입어요** (*ibeoyo*, wear), 받다 → **받아요**, 닫다 → **닫아요** (*dadayo*, close) and 씻다 → **씻어요** (*ssiseoyo*, wash) are all regular. A good dictionary marks the irregular ones.

### "To be": 이에요 / 예요

The copula 이다 ("to be", as in "I am a student") has its own polite form. Use **이에요** after a consonant and **예요** after a vowel: 학생이에요 (*haksaengieyo*, I'm a student), 의사예요 (*uisayeyo*, I'm a doctor). Its negative is **아니에요** (*anieyo*): 학생이 아니에요, "I'm not a student."

## Using the present tense in real sentences

The verb always goes at the end. The same form covers "I eat", "I'm eating" and, with a time word, "I'm going to eat".

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 저는 매일 한국어를 공부해요. | Jeoneun maeil hangugeoreul gongbuhaeyo. | I study Korean every day. |
| 지금 뭐 해요? | Jigeum mwo haeyo? | What are you doing now? |
| 친구를 만나요. | Chingureul mannayo. | I'm meeting a friend. |
| 내일 부산에 가요. | Naeil busane gayo. | I'm going to Busan tomorrow. |
| 김치가 매워요. | Gimchiga maewoyo. | The kimchi is spicy. |
| 오늘 날씨가 추워요. | Oneul nalssiga chuwoyo. | It's cold today. |
| 음악을 들어요. | Eumageul deureoyo. | I'm listening to music. |

### Questions: same form, rising tone

A polite question uses exactly the same verb form. Only your intonation changes, rising at the end like English.

- **커피 마셔요?** (*Keopi masyeoyo?*) "Do you drink coffee?"
- **네, 마셔요.** (*Ne, masyeoyo.*) "Yes, I do."

### Negatives: put 안 in front

To say you don't do something, put **안** (*an*) before the verb: **안 먹어요** (*an meogeoyo*), "I don't eat." For noun + 하다 verbs, 안 goes between the noun and 해요: **공부 안 해요** (*gongbu an haeyo*), "I don't study." Two verbs have their own negative partners instead: 있어요 ↔ **없어요**, and 알아요 (I know) ↔ **몰라요** (I don't know).

To say you **can't** do something, use **못** (*mot*) the same way: **못 가요** (*mot gayo*), "I can't go."

## Where this fits among politeness levels

Korean verb endings also show how formal you're being. The -아요/-어요 style is the one learners should start with, because it's polite without being stiff.

| Style | 가다 | 먹다 | When it's used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formal polite | 갑니다 | 먹습니다 | news, announcements, presentations, the military |
| Polite (this lesson) | 가요 | 먹어요 | everyday talk with strangers, coworkers, older people |
| Casual | 가 | 먹어 | close friends, younger people, family |

Notice that the casual form is just the polite form without **-요**. That's why learning -아요/-어요 first pays off twice. Use casual speech only once someone suggests it, or with people clearly younger than you.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 가아요 | 가요 | ㅏ + 아 merges |
| 보아요 | 봐요 | ㅗ + 아 merges in speech |
| 먹아요 | 먹어요 | 먹 has ㅓ, so -어요 |
| 하요 | 해요 | 하다 always becomes 해요 |
| 덥어요 | 더워요 | ㅂ irregular |
| 듣어요 | 들어요 | ㄷ irregular |
| 모르어요 | 몰라요 | 르 irregular |
| 안 공부해요 | 공부 안 해요 | 안 goes right before 해요 |

## FAQ

**Do adjectives conjugate the same way?** Yes. Korean adjectives like 좋다 (good), 크다 (big) and 춥다 (cold) behave like verbs, so they take -아요/-어요 too: 좋아요, 커요, 추워요. That's why "It's cold" is a single word, 추워요.

**Why is it 봐요 and not 보아요?** 보아요 is the full, uncontracted form and you may see it in older writing or songs, but in speech and normal text everyone says and writes 봐요. The same goes for 와요, 줘요 and 마셔요.

**Is -아요/-어요 polite enough for older people?** For everyday conversation, yes. It's the standard polite style in shops, restaurants and with people you've just met. The formal -ㅂ니다/-습니다 style is for announcements and very formal settings, and honorific verbs like 드세요 (please eat) come later.

**How do I say "I am doing" versus "I do"?** The simple present covers both in most situations: 지금 공부해요 is "I'm studying now". Korean also has a progressive form, -고 있어요 (공부하고 있어요), used when you want to stress that the action is in progress right now.

**How do I know if a verb is irregular?** Learn the common ones in the table above, since they cover most of what beginners need. Most learner dictionaries, including the National Institute of Korean Language's Korean-English learners' dictionary, show the 해요 form next to each verb, which tells you immediately.

**What should I learn next?** Practice with numbers from [Korean numbers](/learn-korean/en/korean-numbers/), like **커피 두 잔 주세요** (*keopi du jan juseyo*, "Two coffees, please"), and start a list of the verbs you'd use to describe your own day.
