English has one word for "and". Korean has several, and the right one depends on **what you are joining**: two nouns, two verbs or adjectives, or two whole sentences. Once you see that split, choosing the right form becomes easy.

> **Quick answer:** To join **nouns**, attach **하고** (*hago*, neutral), **와/과** (*wa/gwa*, formal and written) or **(이)랑** (*(i)rang*, casual) to the first noun: **커피하고 빵** (*keopihago ppang*), "coffee and bread". To join **verbs or adjectives**, replace -다 with **-고** (*-go*): 싸다 + 맛있다 → **싸고 맛있어요** (*ssago masisseoyo*), "it's cheap and tasty". To start a new sentence with "and", use **그리고** (*geurigo*). Don't use 그리고 to join two nouns in everyday speech.

This lesson builds on [Korean particles](/learn-korean/en/korean-particles/) and the [present tense -아요/-어요](/learn-korean/en/korean-present-tense/). Romanization follows the Revised Romanization system.

## The five ways to say "and" at a glance

| Form | Joins | Tone | Example | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 하고 | nouns | neutral, everyday | 커피하고 빵 | keopihago ppang | coffee and bread |
| 와/과 | nouns | formal, written | 커피와 빵 | keopiwa ppang | coffee and bread |
| (이)랑 | nouns | casual, friendly | 커피랑 빵 | keopirang ppang | coffee and bread |
| -고 | verbs, adjectives, clauses | all levels | 싸고 맛있어요 | ssago masisseoyo | it's cheap and tasty |
| 그리고 | sentences | all levels | 그리고 차도 좋아해요 | geurigo chado joahaeyo | and I also like tea |

## Joining nouns: 하고, 와/과, (이)랑

All three are particles, so they attach directly to the **first** noun with no space. The last noun takes whatever particle the sentence needs (을/를, 이/가 and so on), or none at all.

### 하고: the safe everyday choice

하고 never changes. It goes after any noun, whether it ends in a vowel or a consonant, and it fits almost any situation, from talking with friends to ordering in a restaurant.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 물하고 콜라 | mulhago kolla | water and cola |
| 냉면하고 만두 주세요. | Naengmyeonhago mandu juseyo. | Naengmyeon and dumplings, please. |
| 동생하고 저는 서울에 살아요. | Dongsaenghago jeoneun seoure sarayo. | My younger sibling and I live in Seoul. |

The ordering pattern with 주세요 is covered in [please in Korean](/learn-korean/en/korean-please/).

### 와/과: formal and written

와/과 is the form you'll see in textbooks, news, signs, titles and formal speeches. It changes depending on the noun before it:

- **와** after a vowel: 커피**와** (*keopiwa*), 친구**와** (*chinguwa*)
- **과** after a consonant: 빵**과** (*ppanggwa*), 선생님**과** (*seonsaengnimgwa*)

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 서울과 부산 | Seoulgwa Busan | Seoul and Busan |
| 한국어와 영어 | hangugeowa yeongeo | Korean and English |
| 책과 펜 | chaekgwa pen | a book and a pen |

Notice that the rule is the reverse of what many learners expect: the vowel-ending noun takes 와, the consonant-ending noun takes 과. In everyday conversation 와/과 can sound a bit stiff, so most people use 하고 or (이)랑 instead.

### (이)랑: casual and friendly

(이)랑 is very common in spoken Korean, especially with friends and family. It is still fine in polite sentences ending in -요; it just sounds warmer and less formal than 하고.

- **랑** after a vowel: 커피**랑** (*keopirang*), 아빠**랑** (*apparang*)
- **이랑** after a consonant: 빵**이랑** (*ppangirang*), 책**이랑** (*chaegirang*)

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 빵이랑 우유 샀어요. | Ppangirang uyu sasseoyo. | I bought bread and milk. |
| 아빠랑 엄마는 집에 있어요. | Apparang eommaneun jibe isseoyo. | Dad and Mom are at home. |
| 너랑 나 | neorang na | you and me (casual) |

In speech you'll also hear the particle repeated after each item in a list, like **떡볶이랑 김밥이랑** or **물하고 콜라하고**. That's natural in conversation; in writing, use it once.

### Pick one and stay with it

Don't mix the three in the same list. 커피하고 빵이랑 우유 sounds odd; choose one: **커피하고 빵하고 우유** or **커피랑 빵이랑 우유** in speech, **커피와 빵과 우유** or, more naturally, **커피, 빵과 우유** in writing.

## The same particles also mean "with"

하고, 와/과 and (이)랑 also mean "with" when they mark the person you do something with. Adding **같이** (*gachi*, "together") makes this meaning clear.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 친구하고 영화를 봤어요. | Chinguhago yeonghwareul bwasseoyo. | I watched a movie with a friend. |
| 엄마랑 같이 시장에 갔어요. | Eommarang gachi sijange gasseoyo. | I went to the market with my mom. |
| 선생님과 이야기했어요. | Seonsaengnimgwa iyagihaesseoyo. | I talked with the teacher. |

Sometimes a sentence can be read both ways. **친구하고 동생을 만났어요** can mean "I met my friend and my sibling" or "I met my sibling with my friend". Context usually decides; add 같이 if you mean "with".

For past forms like 봤어요 and 갔어요, see the [past tense lesson](/learn-korean/en/korean-past-tense/).

## Joining verbs and adjectives: -고

To say "and" between two actions or descriptions, take the stem of the first verb (drop -다) and add **-고**. The good news: -고 never changes. It's the same after a vowel or a consonant, and even irregular verbs stay regular in front of it.

| Dictionary form | -고 form | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 가다 (to go) | 가고 | gago | go and |
| 먹다 (to eat) | 먹고 | meokgo | eat and |
| 하다 (to do) | 하고 | hago | do and |
| 만들다 (to make) | 만들고 | mandeulgo | make and |
| 듣다 (to listen) | 듣고 | deutgo | listen and |
| 춥다 (to be cold) | 춥고 | chupgo | be cold and |
| 좋다 (to be good) | 좋고 | joko | be good and |
| 학생이다 (to be a student) | 학생이고 | haksaengigo | be a student and |

After a noun ending in a vowel, 이고 often shortens to 고: 의사이고 → **의사고** (*uisago*). Note that 듣다 stays **듣고**, not 들고 (들고 comes from 들다, "to hold").

### Pronunciation tip

After ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ and ㅅ the ㄱ of -고 is pronounced tense, like a stronger "k": 먹고 sounds like [먹꼬] and 씻고 like [씯꼬]. Revised Romanization doesn't show this, so it's still written *meokgo*. After ㅎ, the two sounds merge into ㅋ: 좋고 is pronounced [조코], which is why it's romanized *joko*.

### Use 1: listing ("and")

-고 lists facts, qualities or actions that sit side by side.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 이 식당은 싸고 맛있어요. | I sikdangeun ssago masisseoyo. | This restaurant is cheap and tasty. |
| 날씨가 춥고 바람이 많이 불어요. | Nalssiga chupgo barami mani bureoyo. | It's cold and very windy. |
| 형은 키가 크고 동생은 작아요. | Hyeongeun kiga keugo dongsaengeun jagayo. | My older brother is tall and my younger one is short. |
| 저는 학생이고 제 친구는 회사원이에요. | Jeoneun haksaengigo je chinguneun hoesawonieyo. | I'm a student and my friend is an office worker. |
| 이 사람은 의사고 저 사람은 간호사예요. | I sarameun uisago jeo sarameun ganhosayeyo. | This person is a doctor and that person is a nurse. |

### Use 2: sequence ("and then")

-고 also links actions in time order: first this, then that.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 밥을 먹고 커피를 마셨어요. | Babeul meokgo keopireul masyeosseoyo. | I ate and then had coffee. |
| 숙제를 하고 잘 거예요. | Sukjereul hago jal geoyeyo. | I'll do my homework and then go to bed. |
| 손을 씻고 밥을 먹어요. | Soneul ssitgo babeul meogeoyo. | I wash my hands and then eat. |

To stress "after doing", add **나서**: **밥을 먹고 나서 이를 닦아요** (*Babeul meokgo naseo ireul dakkayo*), "After eating, I brush my teeth."

### Tense goes on the last verb

The -고 form usually stays in its plain form, and the final verb carries the tense and politeness for the whole sentence.

**어제 영화를 보고 저녁을 먹었어요.** (*Eoje yeonghwareul bogo jeonyeogeul meogeosseoyo.*) "Yesterday I watched a movie and had dinner."

The future works the same way, as in 숙제를 하고 잘 거예요 above (see the [future tense](/learn-korean/en/korean-future-tense/)). You can put the past marker before -고 (**-았/었고**) when you list two separate past facts, often contrasting them: **어제는 비가 왔고 오늘은 맑아요** (*Eojeneun biga watgo oneureun malgayo*), "It rained yesterday, and today it's clear." For a simple "did this and then that", use the plain -고.

## -고 vs -아서/어서 for "and then"

Both can link two actions, but they're not interchangeable. Use **-아서/어서** when the second action depends on the first, usually because it happens in the same place or with the same person or thing. Use **-고** when the actions are simply one after another.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 친구를 만나서 영화를 봤어요. | Chingureul mannaseo yeonghwareul bwasseoyo. | I met my friend and (we) watched a movie (together). |
| 친구를 만나고 영화를 봤어요. | Chingureul mannago yeonghwareul bwasseoyo. | I met my friend and then watched a movie (separately). |
| 의자에 앉아서 이야기해요. | Uijae anjaseo iyagihaeyo. | We sit down and talk (while seated). |

Verbs of putting on clothes and getting on transport normally take **-고**, even though the state continues: **코트를 입고 나갔어요** (*Koteureul ipgo nagasseoyo*), "I put on a coat and went out"; **버스를 타고 학교에 가요** (*Beoseureul tago hakgyoe gayo*), "I take the bus to school." -아서/어서 also means "because", which you can review in [because in Korean](/learn-korean/en/korean-because/).

## Joining sentences: 그리고

**그리고** (*geurigo*) starts a new sentence and adds information, just like "And..." or "Also..." in English.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 저는 커피를 좋아해요. 그리고 차도 좋아해요. | Jeoneun keopireul joahaeyo. Geurigo chado joahaeyo. | I like coffee. And I like tea too. |
| 이 방은 넓어요. 그리고 조용해요. | I bangeun neolbeoyo. Geurigo joyonghaeyo. | This room is spacious. And it's quiet. |

For "and then" between sentences, the standard form is **그러고 나서** (*geureogo naseo*): **밥을 먹었어요. 그러고 나서 산책했어요** (*Babeul meogeosseoyo. Geureogo naseo sanchaekaesseoyo*), "I ate. And then I went for a walk." You'll often see 그리고 나서 online, but the National Institute of Korean Language treats it as non-standard.

Two related connectors: **그래서** (*geuraeseo*) means "and so", and **그런데** (*geureonde*) or **하지만** (*hajiman*) mean "but".

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 커피 그리고 빵 주세요. | 커피하고 빵 주세요. | use a particle, not 그리고, between nouns in speech |
| 싸다하고 맛있어요 | 싸고 맛있어요 | verbs and adjectives take -고, not 하고 |
| 빵와 커피 | 빵과 커피 | 과 after a consonant |
| 커피과 빵 | 커피와 빵 | 와 after a vowel |
| 빵랑 우유 | 빵이랑 우유 | 이랑 after a consonant |
| 커피이랑 빵 | 커피랑 빵 | 랑 after a vowel |
| 노래를 들고 | 노래를 듣고 | 듣다 doesn't change before -고 |
| 커피 하고 빵 | 커피하고 빵 | particles attach with no space |

## FAQ

**What is the most common word for "and" in Korean?** It depends on what you join. For nouns in everyday speech, 하고 and (이)랑 are the most common. For verbs and adjectives, -고 is used all the time. 그리고 is the "and" that starts a new sentence.

**Can I use 그리고 between two nouns?** In normal conversation, no. Use 하고, 와/과 or (이)랑. You may see 그리고 between nouns in titles, poems or lyrics for a dramatic pause, but that's a stylistic choice, not the everyday pattern.

**How do I say "And you?" in Korean?** Not with any of the words above. Add **-은/는요** to the person's name or title: **저는 잘 지내요. 민수 씨는요?** (*Jeoneun jal jinaeyo. Minsu ssineunyo?*), "I'm doing well. And you, Minsu?" With friends, **너는?** (*neoneun?*) works.

**Does -고 change in formal or polite speech?** No. Politeness is shown only on the final verb: 싸고 맛있어 (casual), 싸고 맛있어요 (polite), 싸고 맛있습니다 (formal). See [formal vs informal Korean](/learn-korean/en/korean-speech-levels/).

**How do I say "or" in Korean?** For nouns, use **(이)나**: 커피나 차 (*keopina cha*), "coffee or tea"; 빵이나 밥 (*ppangina bap*). For verbs, use **-거나**: 영화를 보거나 책을 읽어요 (*Yeonghwareul bogeona chaegeul ilgeoyo*), "I watch a movie or read a book."

**What is -(으)며?** It's a more formal, mostly written equivalent of -고 that you'll meet in news and essays (그는 가수이며 배우이다, "He is a singer and an actor"). In conversation, stick with -고.

**What's next?** Combine -고 with other patterns you know: [-고 싶어요](/learn-korean/en/korean-want-to/) ("I want to") uses the same -고 ending, and -고 있어요 makes the [present progressive](/learn-korean/en/korean-present-progressive/).
