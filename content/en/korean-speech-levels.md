In Korean you can't just "say something". Every sentence ends in a form that shows how formal the situation is and how close you are to the listener. The same "I'm going" can be 갑니다, 가요 or 가, and choosing the wrong one can sound stiff, cold or rude.

> **Quick answer:** Modern Korean uses three main speech levels. **Formal polite** ends in **-습니다/-ㅂ니다** (갑니다, *gamnida*) and is for presentations, announcements, the military and very formal situations. **Polite** ends in **-요** (가요, *gayo*) and is the safe default with strangers, coworkers and anyone older than you. **Casual**, called **반말** (*banmal*), drops the -요 (가, *ga*) and is only for close friends, younger people and family. Separately, **honorifics** raise the *person you're talking about*: add **-(으)시-** to the verb (가세요, *gaseyo*) and use special words like 드시다 ("to eat", honorific) and 계시다 ("to be", honorific).

If you've followed the [present tense -아요/-어요](/learn-korean/en/korean-present-tense/) lesson, you already know the polite level. This lesson shows how to move one step up and one step down from it, and how to add respect for others. Romanization follows the Revised Romanization system.

## The three speech levels at a glance

| Level | Korean name | Ending | 가다 (to go) | 먹다 (to eat) | 이다 (to be) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formal polite | 합쇼체 / 하십시오체 | -습니다/-ㅂ니다 | 갑니다 (gamnida) | 먹습니다 (meokseumnida) | 학생입니다 (haksaengimnida) |
| Polite | 해요체 | -아요/-어요 | 가요 (gayo) | 먹어요 (meogeoyo) | 학생이에요 (haksaengieyo) |
| Casual (반말) | 해체 | -아/-어 | 가 (ga) | 먹어 (meogeo) | 학생이야 (haksaengiya) |

There is a fourth style you'll see but rarely speak: the **plain style** (해라체), with endings like 간다, 먹는다 and 예쁘다. It's used in books, diaries, essays, news headlines and in Korean dictionaries' example sentences. Older levels such as 하오체 and 하게체 survive mainly in historical dramas and among some older speakers, so beginners can skip them.

Formal and polite are both *respectful*. The difference is the setting: -습니다 sounds official and businesslike, while -요 is warm but still respectful. Most daily conversation with people you don't know well happens in -요.

## Formal polite: -습니다/-ㅂ니다

You'll hear this level from news anchors, airline crews, shop announcements, in job interviews and at the start of speeches. Many fixed expressions also use it, even in otherwise casual conversation: **감사합니다** (*gamsahamnida*, "thank you"), **잘 먹겠습니다** (*jal meokgetseumnida*, said before eating) and **처음 뵙겠습니다** (*cheoeum boepgetseumnida*, "nice to meet you", literally "I'm seeing you for the first time").

### How to conjugate

Drop **-다** from the dictionary form, then:

| Stem ends in | Statement | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| a vowel | + ㅂ니다 | + ㅂ니까? | 가다 → 갑니다 / 갑니까? (gamnida / gamnikka?) |
| a consonant | + 습니다 | + 습니까? | 먹다 → 먹습니다 / 먹습니까? (meokseumnida / meokseumnikka?) |
| ㄹ | drop ㄹ, + ㅂ니다 | drop ㄹ, + ㅂ니까? | 살다 → 삽니다 (samnida), 만들다 → 만듭니다 (mandeumnida) |
| 하다 | 합니다 | 합니까? | 공부하다 → 공부합니다 (gongbuhamnida) |
| noun + 이다 | 입니다 | 입니까? | 학생입니다 (haksaengimnida) |

The ㅂ before ㄴ is pronounced as **m**, so 갑니다 sounds like [감니다] and 먹습니다 like [먹씀니다].

Tense markers go in front of the ending, just as in the polite style:

| Tense | Formal polite | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present | 먹습니다 | meokseumnida | I eat |
| Past | 먹었습니다 | meogeotseumnida | I ate |
| Future | 먹을 겁니다 / 먹겠습니다 | meogeul geomnida / meokgetseumnida | I will eat |
| Negative | 먹지 않습니다 | meokji anseumnida | I don't eat |

**-겠습니다** is very common in formal speech to state an intention politely: **알겠습니다** (*algetseumnida*, "understood"), **열심히 하겠습니다** (*yeolsimhi hagetseumnida*, "I'll do my best").

## Polite: -요 (your default)

This is the level from the [present](/learn-korean/en/korean-present-tense/), [past](/learn-korean/en/korean-past-tense/) and [future tense](/learn-korean/en/korean-future-tense/) lessons: 가요, 갔어요, 갈 거예요. Use it with:

- strangers of any age, including shop staff and taxi drivers
- coworkers and classmates you aren't close to
- anyone older than you, unless they've told you to speak casually
- your teacher, your friend's parents, your landlord

If you're unsure which level to use, use -요. It's never wrong in everyday life. At worst it sounds a little distant with close friends.

## Casual: 반말 (banmal)

**반말** literally means "half speech". To make it, take the polite form and **remove -요**. For most verbs that's all there is to it.

| Polite | Casual | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 가요 | 가 | ga | I go / go |
| 먹어요 | 먹어 | meogeo | I eat |
| 해요 | 해 | hae | I do |
| 갔어요 | 갔어 | gasseo | I went |
| 갈 거예요 | 갈 거야 | gal geoya | I'll go |
| 안 가요 | 안 가 | an ga | I don't go |
| 고마워요 | 고마워 | gomawo | thanks |
| 미안해요 | 미안해 | mianhae | sorry |
| 안녕하세요 | 안녕 | annyeong | hi / bye |

A few words change more than that:

| Polite | Casual | Romanization | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 학생이에요 | 학생이야 | haksaengiya | 이에요 → 이야 after a consonant |
| 친구예요 | 친구야 | chinguya | 예요 → 야 after a vowel |
| 아니에요 | 아니야 | aniya | "it's not / no" |
| 네 / 예 | 응 / 어 | eung / eo | "yes" |
| 아니요 | 아니 | ani | "no" |
| 저 / 제가 / 제 | 나 / 내가 / 내 | na / naega / nae | "I / my" |

In 반말 you call the other person **너** (*neo*, "you") or, much more often, by their name. Note that **당신** (*dangsin*) is not a polite "you": in conversation it's used between spouses or in arguments, so avoid it. In polite speech, use the person's name or title + 씨 or 님 instead.

### Who can you use 반말 with?

Korean relationships depend a lot on age and seniority. Use 반말 with close friends of the same age, people clearly younger than you once you know each other, and children. Family use varies: most people use 반말 with siblings and many with their parents, but grandparents usually get -요 or honorifics.

The usual way to switch is for the **older or more senior person** to offer: **말 놓으세요** (*Mal noeuseyo*, "Please speak casually with me") or **말 편하게 하세요** (*Mal pyeonhage haseyo*). The younger person can ask **말 놓아도 돼요?** (*Mal noado dwaeyo?*, "May I speak casually?"). Switching to 반말 with someone older without this step can come across as rude.

## Honorifics: respecting the person you're talking about

Speech levels show respect to the **listener**. Honorifics show respect to the **subject of the sentence**, usually someone older or higher in status. The two systems combine: you can talk *to* a friend in 반말 *about* your grandmother with honorifics.

### -(으)시-

Add **-시-** after a vowel stem and **-으시-** after a consonant stem. In the polite style, -시 + -어요 contracts to **-세요**.

| Verb | Polite honorific | Past | Formal polite | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 가다 | 가세요 (gaseyo) | 가셨어요 (gasyeosseoyo) | 가십니다 (gasimnida) | (someone respected) goes |
| 오다 | 오세요 (oseyo) | 오셨어요 (osyeosseoyo) | 오십니다 (osimnida) | comes |
| 읽다 | 읽으세요 (ilgeuseyo) | 읽으셨어요 (ilgeusyeosseoyo) | 읽으십니다 (ilgeusimnida) | reads |
| 하다 | 하세요 (haseyo) | 하셨어요 (hasyeosseoyo) | 하십니다 (hasimnida) | does |

This is the same -세요 you see in **안녕하세요** and in polite requests like **앉으세요** (*anjeuseyo*, "please sit down"). Questions to strangers almost always use it: **어디 가세요?** (*Eodi gaseyo?*, "Where are you going?").

**Never use -시- about yourself.** 제가 가세요 is wrong. Say 제가 가요.

### Special honorific verbs and nouns

Some common words have a separate honorific form instead of (or in addition to) -시-.

| Plain | Honorific | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 먹다 / 마시다 | 드시다 | deusida | to eat / drink |
| 자다 | 주무시다 | jumusida | to sleep |
| 있다 (be somewhere) | 계시다 | gyesida | to be, to stay |
| 말하다 | 말씀하시다 | malsseumhasida | to speak |
| 아프다 | 편찮으시다 | pyeonchaneusida | to be ill |
| 이름 | 성함 | seongham | name |
| 나이 | 연세 | yeonse | age |
| 집 | 댁 | daek | home, house |
| 생일 | 생신 | saengsin | birthday |

Several everyday phrases are built from these: **맛있게 드세요** (*Masitge deuseyo*, "Enjoy your meal"), **안녕히 주무세요** (*Annyeonghi jumuseyo*, "Good night"), **안녕히 계세요** (*Annyeonghi gyeseyo*, "Goodbye", said when you leave and the other person stays). See [Korean greetings](/learn-korean/en/korean-greetings/) for more.

"Do you have time?" uses the regular form: **시간 있으세요?** (*Sigan isseuseyo?*). 계시다 is only for a person being somewhere: **할아버지께서 댁에 계세요** (*Harabeojikkeseo daege gyeseyo*, "Grandfather is at home").

### Honorific particles

| Plain particle | Honorific | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 이/가 | 께서 | 선생님께서 오셨어요. (Seonsaengnimkkeseo osyeosseoyo.) The teacher has come. |
| 은/는 | 께서는 | 할머니께서는 주무세요. (Halmeonikkeseoneun jumuseyo.) Grandmother is sleeping. |
| 에게 / 한테 | 께 | 선생님께 질문했어요. (Seonsaengnimkke jilmunhaesseoyo.) I asked the teacher a question. |

께서 is common in writing and formal speech. In everyday talk many people keep the normal particle and just use the honorific verb: 선생님이 오셨어요. (For the basic particles, see [Korean particles](/learn-korean/en/korean-particles/).)

### Humble forms: lowering yourself

The other side of honorifics is speaking modestly about yourself.

| Neutral | Humble | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 나 | 저 | jeo | I |
| 우리 | 저희 | jeohui | we, our |
| 주다 | 드리다 | deurida | to give (to someone respected) |
| 묻다 | 여쭈다 / 여쭤보다 | yeojjuda / yeojjwoboda | to ask (someone respected) |
| 만나다 / 보다 | 뵙다 / 뵈다 | boepda / boeda | to meet, to see (someone respected) |

Example: **할머니께 선물을 드렸어요** (*Halmeonikke seonmureul deuryeosseoyo*, "I gave my grandmother a present").

## One sentence, four ways

| Situation | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| To a close friend | 밥 먹었어? | Bap meogeosseo? |
| To a coworker your age | 밥 먹었어요? | Bap meogeosseoyo? |
| To your boss | 식사하셨어요? | Siksahasyeosseoyo? |
| Very formal, to an elder | 진지 드셨습니까? | Jinji deusyeotseumnikka? |

All four mean "Have you eaten?", a common greeting. Notice how both the ending and the vocabulary change as respect goes up.

| Situation | "What's your name?" |
|---|---|
| To a child or friend | 이름이 뭐야? (Ireumi mwoya?) |
| To a peer | 이름이 뭐예요? (Ireumi mwoyeyo?) |
| To an older adult or customer | 성함이 어떻게 되세요? (Seonghami eotteoke doeseyo?) |

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 제가 가세요. | 제가 가요. | never honor yourself with -시- |
| 할머니가 자요. | 할머니께서 주무세요. | use the honorific verb for elders |
| 당신은 어디 가요? | (이름)씨는 어디 가요? / 어디 가세요? | 당신 isn't a polite "you" |
| 나는 학생이에요. | 저는 학생이에요. | use 저 with polite endings |
| 커피 나오셨습니다. | 커피 나왔습니다. | -시- is for people, not things |
| 반말 with an older stranger | -요 or -(으)세요 | wait to be invited to speak casually |

The coffee example is something you may actually hear in cafés. Staff over-apply -시- to sound extra polite, but standard grammar considers it incorrect because coffee isn't a person.

## Practice

Change each polite sentence to **formal polite** and to **반말**.

| Polite | Formal polite | Casual |
|---|---|---|
| 감사해요. | 감사합니다. (Gamsahamnida.) | 고마워. (Gomawo.) |
| 학생이에요. | 학생입니다. (Haksaengimnida.) | 학생이야. (Haksaengiya.) |
| 서울에 살아요. | 서울에 삽니다. (Seoure samnida.) | 서울에 살아. (Seoure sara.) |
| 어제 영화를 봤어요. | 어제 영화를 봤습니다. (Eoje yeonghwareul bwatseumnida.) | 어제 영화를 봤어. (Eoje yeonghwareul bwasseo.) |
| 내일 안 가요. | 내일 안 갑니다. (Naeil an gamnida.) | 내일 안 가. (Naeil an ga.) |

Casual "thanks" is 고마워, not 감사해: 감사하다 is the more formal verb.

Now make each sentence honorific:

| Sentence | Honorific |
|---|---|
| 아버지가 신문을 읽어요. (Dad reads the newspaper.) | 아버지께서 신문을 읽으세요. (Abeojikkeseo sinmuneul ilgeuseyo.) |
| 할머니가 밥을 먹어요. (Grandma eats.) | 할머니께서 진지를 드세요. (Halmeonikkeseo jinjireul deuseyo.) |
| 선생님이 학교에 있어요. (The teacher is at school.) | 선생님께서 학교에 계세요. (Seonsaengnimkkeseo hakgyoe gyeseyo.) |
| 사장님이 말했어요. (The boss said.) | 사장님께서 말씀하셨어요. (Sajangnimkkeseo malsseumhasyeosseoyo.) |

## FAQ

**Which level should a beginner learn first?** The polite -요 style. It covers nearly every situation a learner meets, and formal and casual forms are easy to derive from it once you know it.

**Is -습니다 more polite than -요?** It's more *formal*, not necessarily more respectful. Using -습니다 with a friend's mother at dinner can sound stiff, while -요 with honorifics (드세요, 계세요) sounds both warm and respectful.

**Do K-dramas and K-pop use 반말?** A lot. Characters who are friends, couples or siblings speak 반말, and song lyrics are mostly casual or plain style. That's why learners pick up 반말 quickly, but be careful copying it with people you've just met.

**Can I use 반말 with someone younger than me right away?** Usually it's still polite to start with -요 with adults you've just met, even if they're younger. Many people switch once they're comfortable or once the older person suggests it.

**Do I use honorifics for my own parents?** When talking about them to others, especially in formal situations, yes: 아버지께서 회사에 다니세요 ("My father works at a company"). In daily conversation with them, many people use -요 or 반말 with some honorific verbs.

**What's next?** Now that you can choose the right level, a good next step is suggesting and asking with -(으)ㄹ까요? (갈까요?, "Shall we go?"), and practicing negatives in every level with [Korean negation](/learn-korean/en/korean-negation/).
