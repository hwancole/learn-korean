"Hello" is the first word everyone wants in Korean, and it's also your first lesson in politeness. Korean has different speech levels, and the greeting you pick tells people how formal you're being.

> **Quick answer:** The standard way to say hello in Korean is **안녕하세요 (annyeonghaseyo)**. It's polite, safe with strangers, shop staff, teachers and coworkers, and works at any time of day. With close friends or children you can say **안녕 (annyeong)**. In very formal settings, like a speech or a job interview, use **안녕하십니까 (annyeonghasimnikka)**.

If you can't read Hangul yet, start with [Learn Hangul in One Hour](/learn-korean/en/hangul-alphabet/). Romanization is included below, but the pronunciation tips make more sense once you know the letters.

## First, the three politeness levels you'll hear

You don't need grammar to use greetings, but it helps to recognize three endings. They show up in almost every phrase on this page.

| Level | Typical ending | Example | Use it with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formal polite | -ㅂ니다 / -습니다 (-mnida / -seumnida) | 감사합니다 | announcements, presentations, customer service, your boss in a meeting |
| Polite | -요 (-yo) | 고마워요 | most everyday situations with adults you don't know well |
| Casual (반말, banmal) | no ending | 고마워 | close friends your age or younger, children, family |

**Rule of thumb for learners:** when in doubt, use the polite **-요** form or the formal **-ㅂ니다** form. Speaking too politely is never a problem. Using casual speech with a stranger or an older person can come across as rude.

## Hello

| Korean | Romanization | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| 안녕하세요 | annyeonghaseyo | the everyday polite hello, any time of day |
| 안녕하십니까 | annyeonghasimnikka | very formal: military, news anchors, formal speeches, greeting customers |
| 안녕 | annyeong | casual hello with close friends and children |
| 여보세요 | yeoboseyo | "hello?" on the phone only |

안녕하세요 literally asks "Are you at peace?" (안녕 means peace or well-being), but nobody thinks about the literal meaning. Treat it as "hello". You can answer with the same word.

**Pronunciation tip:** say it in five even beats, *an-nyeong-ha-se-yo*. The ㄴ at the end of 안 runs into the ㄴ of 녕, so you hear a longer "n". In fast speech the ㅎ (h) softens, and it can sound close to *an-nyeong-a-se-yo*.

Korean has no separate everyday words for "good morning" or "good evening". 안녕하세요 covers all of them. You may see 좋은 아침이에요 (joeun achimieyo, "it's a good morning"), but it's much less common than in English.

## Goodbye: who is leaving?

This is the part that surprises learners. Korean has two polite goodbyes, and the right one depends on **who is leaving**.

| Korean | Romanization | Literal meaning | Say it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 안녕히 가세요 | annyeonghi gaseyo | "go in peace" | the **other person is leaving** (you stay) |
| 안녕히 계세요 | annyeonghi gyeseyo | "stay in peace" | **you are leaving** (they stay) |
| 잘 가 | jal ga | "go well" | casual, to a friend who is leaving |
| 안녕 | annyeong | "bye" | casual, either situation |

Example: when you leave a shop, you say **안녕히 계세요** to the staff, because they stay. The staff say **안녕히 가세요** to you, because you're going. If you both walk off in different directions, both of you say 안녕히 가세요.

A trick to remember it: 가 (ga) is from the verb 가다 (to go), so 가세요 goes to the person who goes.

## Thank you

| Korean | Romanization | Level |
|---|---|---|
| 감사합니다 | gamsahamnida | formal polite, the safest choice |
| 고맙습니다 | gomapseumnida | formal polite, slightly warmer |
| 고마워요 | gomawoyo | polite |
| 고마워 | gomawo | casual |

감사합니다 and 고맙습니다 are equally polite. 감사 comes from Chinese characters and 고맙다 is a native Korean word, so some people feel 고맙습니다 sounds a little softer. Either is fine everywhere.

**Pronunciation tip:** the ㅂ in 합니다 is pronounced like ㅁ before ㄴ, so 감사합니다 sounds like *gam-sa-ham-ni-da*. The same rule makes 고맙습니다 sound like *go-map-sseum-ni-da*.

To reply to "thank you", Koreans usually say **아니에요 (anieyo)**, "not at all", or **별말씀을요 (byeolmalsseumeullyo)**, "don't mention it". In shops you'll often hear nothing back except another 감사합니다, and that's normal.

## Sorry and excuse me

| Korean | Romanization | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| 죄송합니다 | joesonghamnida | formal apology; to strangers, staff, older people |
| 미안합니다 | mianhamnida | polite apology, less deferential than 죄송합니다 |
| 미안해요 | mianhaeyo | polite, everyday |
| 미안해 | mianhae | casual, to friends |
| 실례합니다 | sillyehamnida | "excuse me" before interrupting or squeezing past someone |
| 저기요 | jeogiyo | "excuse me!" to get the attention of a server or a stranger |

In a restaurant, 저기요 is the normal way to call the staff to your table. It isn't rude. If you bump into someone on the subway, a quick 죄송합니다 is the standard response.

## Nice to meet you

| Korean | Romanization | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 처음 뵙겠습니다 | cheoeum boepgetseumnida | very polite: "I'm seeing you for the first time" |
| 만나서 반갑습니다 | mannaseo bangapseumnida | formal: "glad to meet you" |
| 만나서 반가워요 | mannaseo bangawoyo | polite |
| 반가워 | bangawo | casual, to a new friend your age |

A typical first meeting in polite Korean looks like this:

- A: 안녕하세요. 처음 뵙겠습니다. 저는 민지예요. (*Annyeonghaseyo. Cheoeum boepgetseumnida. Jeoneun Minjiyeyo.*) "Hello. Nice to meet you. I'm Minji."
- B: 안녕하세요. 저는 알렉스예요. 만나서 반갑습니다. (*Annyeonghaseyo. Jeoneun Allekseuyeyo. Mannaseo bangapseumnida.*) "Hello. I'm Alex. Nice to meet you."

저는 … 예요/이에요 means "I am …". Use **예요** after a name ending in a vowel (민지예요) and **이에요** after a name ending in a consonant (준혁이에요). We'll cover this pattern in its own lesson.

## "How are you?" in Korean

English speakers often look for a direct "How are you?". Korean doesn't use one as a routine greeting with strangers. 안녕하세요 already does that job. With people you know, these are common:

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 잘 지냈어요? | jal jinaesseoyo? | "Have you been well?" (when you haven't seen someone for a while) |
| 잘 지내셨어요? | jal jinaesyeosseoyo? | the same, more respectful, for older people |
| 잘 지냈어? | jal jinaesseo? | casual |
| 식사하셨어요? | siksahasyeosseoyo? | "Have you eaten?", a friendly check-in, not an invitation |

A natural answer to 잘 지냈어요? is **네, 잘 지냈어요 (ne, jal jinaesseoyo)**, "Yes, I've been well", optionally followed by **…씨는요? (…ssineunyo?)**, "And you, …?"

## Yes, no and a few survival extras

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 네 / 예 | ne / ye | yes (also "I see", "OK") |
| 아니요 | aniyo | no |
| 잘 자요 | jal jayo | good night (polite) |
| 안녕히 주무세요 | annyeonghi jumuseyo | good night (to older people, very respectful) |
| 또 만나요 | tto mannayo | see you again |
| 수고하셨습니다 | sugohasyeotseumnida | "thanks for your hard work", said to coworkers at the end of a task or the day |

네 doesn't always mean you agree. Koreans also use it to show they're listening, like "uh-huh" or "right" in English.

## Bowing and body language

Greetings in Korea usually come with a bow. For most daily situations a small nod or a slight bow from the waist is enough. A deeper bow shows more respect, for example to a teacher or a much older person. Handshakes are common in business, often with a slight bow at the same time, and the younger person may support their right forearm with their left hand as a sign of respect.

## FAQ

**Is 안녕하세요 too formal for friends?** Not rude, just a little distant. Friends your age who are close to you usually switch to 안녕, but only use casual speech once they use it with you or suggest it.

**What does "annyeong" mean in K-dramas?** 안녕 is the casual form and can mean both "hi" and "bye". The context tells you which.

**How do I say "thank you very much"?** Add 정말 (jeongmal, "really") or 대단히 (daedanhi, "greatly"): 정말 감사합니다 (jeongmal gamsahamnida) or, very formally, 대단히 감사합니다 (daedanhi gamsahamnida).

**Why do romanizations differ online?** You'll see "annyeonghaseyo", "annyong haseyo" and "anyonghaseyo". This blog uses Revised Romanization, the official system used on road signs in South Korea. The best fix is to read the Hangul directly.

**What should I learn next?** Korean numbers. You'll need them for prices, phone numbers and telling the time.
