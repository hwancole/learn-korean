Korean numbers have a reputation for being confusing, and there's one reason: Korean uses **two complete sets of numbers**. Once you know which set goes with which situation, the rest is memorization, and the patterns are very regular.

> **Quick answer:** **Sino-Korean numbers** (일, 이, 삼… *il, i, sam*) come from Chinese and are used for **money, dates, minutes, phone numbers, floors and anything above 99**. **Native Korean numbers** (하나, 둘, 셋… *hana, dul, set*) are used for **hours, age and counting things or people** with a counter word. So 3:30 is **세 시 삼십 분** (*se si samsip bun*): native for the hour, Sino-Korean for the minutes.

If you can't read Hangul yet, start with [Learn Hangul in One Hour](/learn-korean/en/hangul-alphabet/). Romanization below follows the Revised Romanization system used on South Korean road signs.

## Sino-Korean numbers (일, 이, 삼)

These are the easiest to build, because everything above 10 is made by combining a few words, like stacking blocks.

| Number | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 영 / 공 | yeong / gong |
| 1 | 일 | il |
| 2 | 이 | i |
| 3 | 삼 | sam |
| 4 | 사 | sa |
| 5 | 오 | o |
| 6 | 육 | yuk |
| 7 | 칠 | chil |
| 8 | 팔 | pal |
| 9 | 구 | gu |
| 10 | 십 | sip |

Now the pattern. Say the tens digit, then 십, then the ones digit:

| Number | Built as | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11 | 10 + 1 | 십일 | sibil |
| 15 | 10 + 5 | 십오 | sibo |
| 20 | 2 × 10 | 이십 | isip |
| 23 | 2 × 10 + 3 | 이십삼 | isipsam |
| 47 | 4 × 10 + 7 | 사십칠 | sasipchil |
| 99 | 9 × 10 + 9 | 구십구 | gusipgu |

There is no extra word like "-teen" or "-ty". If you can count to 10, you can count to 99.

**Pronunciation tip:** 16 is written 십육 but pronounced *sim-nyuk* [심뉵], and 26 sounds like *i-sim-nyuk*. Also, the ㅂ at the end of 십 links to a following vowel, so 십일 is *si-bil*, not *sip-il*.

### Zero: 영 or 공?

Both mean zero. **영 (yeong)** is used in math, scores and temperatures. **공 (gong)** is used when reading out digits one by one, especially phone numbers.

## Native Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋)

This set only goes up to 99, and each multiple of ten has its own word, so there's more to memorize.

| Number | Korean | Romanization | Before a counter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 하나 | hana | 한 (han) |
| 2 | 둘 | dul | 두 (du) |
| 3 | 셋 | set | 세 (se) |
| 4 | 넷 | net | 네 (ne) |
| 5 | 다섯 | daseot | 다섯 |
| 6 | 여섯 | yeoseot | 여섯 |
| 7 | 일곱 | ilgop | 일곱 |
| 8 | 여덟 | yeodeol | 여덟 |
| 9 | 아홉 | ahop | 아홉 |
| 10 | 열 | yeol | 열 |
| 20 | 스물 | seumul | 스무 (seumu) |

The tens:

| Number | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 30 | 서른 | seoreun |
| 40 | 마흔 | maheun |
| 50 | 쉰 | swin |
| 60 | 예순 | yesun |
| 70 | 일흔 | ilheun |
| 80 | 여든 | yeodeun |
| 90 | 아흔 | aheun |

Combine them the same way as in English: 열하나 (*yeolhana*, 11), 스물다섯 (*seumuldaseot*, 25), 서른둘 (*seoreundul*, 32).

### The shortened forms matter

Look at the last column in the first table. When a native number comes right before a counter word, **1, 2, 3, 4 and 20 lose their last sound**:

- 하나 → **한** 개 (*han gae*, one item)
- 둘 → **두** 명 (*du myeong*, two people)
- 셋 → **세** 시 (*se si*, three o'clock)
- 넷 → **네** 잔 (*ne jan*, four cups)
- 스물 → **스무** 살 (*seumu sal*, twenty years old)

This also applies inside bigger numbers: 열한 개 (11 items), 스물두 명 (22 people). It's one of the most common beginner mistakes, so practice these five until they're automatic.

## Which system do I use? The cheat sheet

| Situation | System | Example | Romanization |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prices | Sino-Korean | 오천 원 (₩5,000) | ocheon won |
| Phone numbers | Sino-Korean | 공일공… | gongilgong… |
| Dates (year, month, day) | Sino-Korean | 시월 이십삼 일 | siwol isipsam il |
| Minutes and seconds | Sino-Korean | 삼십 분 | samsip bun |
| Floors, rooms, bus lines | Sino-Korean | 삼 층, 사백일 번 버스 | sam cheung, sabaegil beon beoseu |
| Anything above 99 | Sino-Korean | 백 개 | baek gae |
| Hours on the clock | Native | 세 시 | se si |
| Age (everyday speech) | Native | 스물다섯 살 | seumuldaseot sal |
| Counting things and people | Native | 커피 두 잔 | keopi du jan |
| Number of times | Native | 한 번 | han beon |
| Duration in hours | Native | 두 시간 | du sigan |

A useful way to remember it: **native numbers count, Sino-Korean numbers measure and label.** When you're counting how many of something there are, use native numbers. When a number works like a label or a measurement (a date, a price, a floor, a phone number), use Sino-Korean.

For large counts you'll hear both. Sino-Korean becomes more common as numbers grow, and above 99 it's the only option, since native Korean numbers stop at 아흔아홉 (99).

## Telling the time

Time mixes both systems, which is why it trips people up. **Hours are native, minutes are Sino-Korean.**

| Time | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 1:00 | 한 시 | han si |
| 2:15 | 두 시 십오 분 | du si sibo bun |
| 3:30 | 세 시 삼십 분 / 세 시 반 | se si samsip bun / se si ban |
| 7:45 | 일곱 시 사십오 분 | ilgop si sasibo bun |
| 12:05 | 열두 시 오 분 | yeoldu si o bun |

- 반 (*ban*) means "half", so 세 시 반 is "half past three".
- **오전** (*ojeon*) is a.m. and **오후** (*ohu*) is p.m. They go before the time: 오후 두 시 (*ohu du si*), 2 p.m.
- To ask the time: **지금 몇 시예요?** (*jigeum myeot siyeyo?*), "What time is it now?"

## Counter words you'll use every day

Korean usually puts a counter word after the number, a bit like "two **cups** of coffee" or "three **sheets** of paper" in English, but for almost everything. The word order is **noun + number + counter**.

| Counter | Used for | Example | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 개 | general things | 사과 세 개 | sagwa se gae | three apples |
| 명 | people | 학생 두 명 | haksaeng du myeong | two students |
| 분 | people (honorific) | 두 분 | du bun | two people (polite) |
| 잔 | cups, glasses | 커피 한 잔 | keopi han jan | one cup of coffee |
| 병 | bottles | 물 두 병 | mul du byeong | two bottles of water |
| 마리 | animals | 고양이 한 마리 | goyangi han mari | one cat |
| 권 | books | 책 네 권 | chaek ne gwon | four books |
| 장 | flat things, tickets | 표 두 장 | pyo du jang | two tickets |
| 살 | age | 열 살 | yeol sal | ten years old |

If you forget the right counter, **개** works for most objects, and people will understand you.

In a restaurant, staff often ask **몇 분이세요?** (*myeot buniseyo?*), "How many people?" You can answer simply: **두 명이요** (*du myeongiyo*), "Two people."

## Money and big numbers

Korean groups big numbers by **ten thousand**, not by thousand. This is the biggest mental shift for English speakers.

| Number | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 백 | baek |
| 1,000 | 천 | cheon |
| 10,000 | 만 | man |
| 100,000 | 십만 | simman |
| 1,000,000 | 백만 | baengman |
| 10,000,000 | 천만 | cheonman |
| 100,000,000 | 억 | eok |

So 50,000 is not "fifty thousand" but "five ten-thousands": **오만** (*oman*). One million is "one hundred ten-thousands": **백만**.

Note that 100, 1,000 and 10,000 are just 백, 천 and 만, without 일 (one) in front.

Prices in won (원) are read with Sino-Korean numbers:

| Price | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| ₩500 | 오백 원 | obaek won |
| ₩4,500 | 사천오백 원 | sacheon obaek won |
| ₩12,000 | 만 이천 원 | man icheon won |
| ₩35,000 | 삼만 오천 원 | samman ocheon won |
| ₩150,000 | 십오만 원 | siboman won |

Korean banknotes come in 1,000, 5,000, 10,000 and 50,000 won, so the ten-thousand unit 만 comes up constantly. To ask a price: **얼마예요?** (*eolmayeyo?*), "How much is it?"

## Phone numbers

Phone numbers are read digit by digit with Sino-Korean numbers, using **공** for zero. The dash is usually read as **에** (*e*).

010-1234-5678 → **공일공에 일이삼사에 오육칠팔** (*gongilgong-e iisamsa-e oyukchilpal*)

## Dates

Dates go from largest to smallest: year (년), month (월), day (일), all with Sino-Korean numbers.

**2026년 10월 23일** → 이천이십육 년 시월 이십삼 일 (*icheon isimnyuk nyeon siwol isipsam il*)

Two months are irregular, because a final consonant drops for easier pronunciation:

- June is **유월** (*yuwol*), not 육월
- October is **시월** (*siwol*), not 십월

The other months are regular: 일월 (January), 이월 (February), 삼월 (March), and so on up to 십이월 (December).

## Age

In everyday conversation, age uses native numbers with **살** (*sal*): 스물다섯 살 (25 years old), 서른 살 (30). On forms and in formal writing you'll see Sino-Korean numbers with **세** (*se*): 25세.

- **몇 살이에요?** (*myeot sarieyo?*), "How old are you?"
- **스물다섯 살이에요.** (*seumuldaseot sarieyo.*), "I'm 25."

You may have read about "Korean age", where people counted themselves one year old at birth. Since June 2023, South Korean law uses international age (만 나이) for official and legal purposes, so the age on your passport is the one you'd normally give.

## Why there's no 4th floor (sometimes)

사 (4) sounds the same as the Sino-Korean word for death (死), so some Korean buildings, especially hospitals and older apartments, label the 4th floor as **F** in elevators, or skip it. It's a superstition similar to the number 13 in Western countries.

## FAQ

**Why does Korean have two number systems?** Native Korean numbers are the original Korean words. Sino-Korean numbers came into Korean from Chinese centuries ago, along with a large part of the vocabulary. Over time each set settled into its own jobs.

**Which set should I learn first?** Learn both from 1 to 10 right away, since you'll need native numbers for counting and Sino-Korean ones for prices on day one. Then learn the Sino-Korean pattern up to 99, which takes minutes, and the native tens (스물, 서른, 마흔…) after that.

**Do Koreans use native numbers above 99?** No. Native Korean numbers stop at 99. For 100 and above, everyone uses Sino-Korean: 백 명 (100 people), 백 원 (₩100).

**Is it "한 시" or "일 시" for one o'clock?** 한 시. Hours always take native numbers. 일 시 sounds wrong to native speakers.

**What's the difference between 시 and 시간?** 시 is a point on the clock (세 시, three o'clock). 시간 is a length of time (세 시간, three hours). Both use native numbers.

**What should I learn next?** Go back over [basic Korean greetings](/learn-korean/en/korean-greetings/) and combine them with numbers to order at a café: 안녕하세요, 아메리카노 두 잔 주세요 (*Annyeonghaseyo, amerikano du jan juseyo*), "Hello, two Americanos, please."
