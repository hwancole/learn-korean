If you watch K-dramas or follow K-pop, you have already heard 오빠 (*oppa*) and 언니 (*eonni*) many times. What surprises most learners is that Korean has no single word for "brother" or "sister": the word you use depends on whether the sibling is older or younger, and on **your own gender**. Once you understand that one idea, the rest of the family vocabulary falls into place.

> **Quick answer:** For older siblings, the word depends on who is speaking. A **woman** calls her older brother **오빠** (*oppa*) and her older sister **언니** (*eonni*). A **man** calls his older brother **형** (*hyeong*, often written "hyung") and his older sister **누나** (*nuna*, often written "noona"). Any younger sibling is **동생** (*dongsaeng*): **남동생** (*namdongsaeng*) is a younger brother and **여동생** (*yeodongsaeng*) a younger sister. Mom and dad are **엄마** (*eomma*) and **아빠** (*appa*).

Romanization in this lesson follows the Revised Romanization system, which is why you will see *hyeong* and *nuna* rather than the fan spellings "hyung" and "noona." Example sentences use the polite -아/어요 style from [the present tense](/learn-korean/en/korean-present-tense/), and the lesson on [speech levels](/learn-korean/en/korean-speech-levels/) explains why some family words have extra-respectful forms.

## Older and younger siblings

| Relationship | Female speaker says | Male speaker says |
|---|---|---|
| Older brother | 오빠 (oppa) | 형 (hyeong) |
| Older sister | 언니 (eonni) | 누나 (nuna) |
| Younger brother | 남동생 (namdongsaeng) | 남동생 (namdongsaeng) |
| Younger sister | 여동생 (yeodongsaeng) | 여동생 (yeodongsaeng) |
| Younger sibling (either) | 동생 (dongsaeng) | 동생 (dongsaeng) |

The four "older" words are also what you **call** the person. A younger sister shouts 오빠! to get her older brother's attention, and a younger brother says 누나! to his older sister. Younger siblings, on the other hand, are usually called by their name, not "동생."

Words for siblings as a group:

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 형제 | hyeongje | brothers; also siblings in general |
| 자매 | jamae | sisters |
| 남매 | nammae | brother(s) and sister(s) |
| 첫째 | cheotjjae | the first child |
| 둘째 | duljjae | the second child |
| 막내 | mangnae | the youngest |
| 외동 | oedong | only child |

**막내** (*mangnae*, often spelled "maknae" by fans) is also what K-pop fans call the youngest member of a group.

Many adults keep saying 엄마 and 아빠 to their own parents. In formal situations, or when talking about your parents to a teacher or boss, 아버지 and 어머니 sound more mature.

### Mom's side vs. dad's side

Korean marks the mother's side of the family with **외** (*oe*), which literally means "outside." Grandparents on the father's side can take **친** (*chin*), meaning "one's own."

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 친할머니 | chinhalmeoni | grandmother (father's mother) |
| 친할아버지 | chinharabeoji | grandfather (father's father) |
| 외할머니 | oehalmeoni | grandmother (mother's mother) |
| 외할아버지 | oeharabeoji | grandfather (mother's father) |

In everyday conversation, people simply say 할머니 to their grandmother's face and add 친 or 외 only when they need to make clear which one they mean.

## Aunts, uncles and cousins

This is where Korean is far more precise than English. Each aunt and uncle has a different word depending on whose sibling they are.

| Korean | Romanization | Who it is |
|---|---|---|
| 큰아버지 | keunabeoji | father's older brother |
| 큰어머니 | keuneomeoni | his wife |
| 작은아버지 | jageunabeoji | father's younger brother (married) |
| 작은어머니 | jageuneomeoni | his wife |
| 삼촌 | samchon | father's younger brother (unmarried); casually, any uncle |
| 고모 | gomo | father's sister |
| 고모부 | gomobu | her husband |
| 외삼촌 | oesamchon | mother's brother |
| 외숙모 | oesungmo | his wife |
| 이모 | imo | mother's sister |
| 이모부 | imobu | her husband |
| 사촌 | sachon | cousin |
| 조카 | joka | niece or nephew |

In casual speech, children often say **큰아빠** (*keunappa*) and **작은아빠** (*jageunappa*) instead of 큰아버지 and 작은아버지. The suffix **부** (*bu*) in 고모부 and 이모부 means "husband of," which makes those two easy to remember.

## Respectful forms with 님

Adding **님** (*nim*) makes a family word more respectful. Use these forms for **someone else's** family, or for in-laws.

| Plain | Respectful | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 아버지 | 아버님 | abeonim |
| 어머니 | 어머님 | eomeonim |
| 형 | 형님 | hyeongnim |
| 누나 | 누님 | nunim |
| 아들 | 아드님 | adeunim |
| 딸 | 따님 | ttanim |

When you ask about someone's parents, say **부모님** or **어머님**, not 엄마: 어머님은 잘 지내세요? (*Eomeonimeun jal jinaeseyo?*, "How is your mother doing?"). 형님 is also how gangster and office dramas show men addressing an older man they respect. The older form of 오빠, **오라버니** (*orabeoni*), mostly appears in historical dramas.

## "Our mom": using 우리 and 저희

Koreans normally say **우리** (*uri*, "our") rather than "my" with family words, even when they are an only child:

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 우리 엄마 | uri eomma | my mom |
| 우리 언니 | uri eonni | my older sister |
| 우리 가족 | uri gajok | my family |
| 저희 부모님 | jeohui bumonim | my parents (humble) |

내 엄마 (*nae eomma*, "my mom") is grammatical but sounds unusually emphatic. In polite speech to a senior, the humble **저희** (*jeohui*) replaces 우리.

## Family words for people who are not family

Korean uses family terms far beyond the family, and this is the reason 오빠 and 언니 are everywhere in dramas.

- **오빠, 언니, 형, 누나** are used for slightly older friends, classmates, coworkers you are close to, and idols. A woman calls an older male friend 오빠; a man calls an older female friend 누나. A boyfriend who is older is also usually called 오빠.
- The name comes **before** the title: **지민 오빠** (*Jimin oppa*), **수진 언니** (*Sujin eonni*), never "오빠 지민."
- **이모** (*imo*, "aunt") is a friendly way to call a middle-aged woman working in a small restaurant: 이모, 여기 물 좀 주세요 (*Imo, yeogi mul jom juseyo*, "Excuse me, could we get some water here?").
- **삼촌** (*samchon*) is what young children often call a younger adult man they know, such as a family friend.
- **할머니** and **할아버지** are polite ways to address elderly strangers.

For strangers of your own generation or older in general, Koreans more often use **저기요** (*jeogiyo*, "excuse me") than any family term. The [please](/learn-korean/en/korean-please/) lesson explains 좀 주세요.

## Talking about your family

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 가족이 몇 명이에요? | Gajogi myeot myeongieyo? | How many people are in your family? |
| 우리 가족은 네 명이에요. | Uri gajogeun ne myeongieyo. | There are four people in my family. |
| 형제가 어떻게 되세요? | Hyeongjega eotteoke doeseyo? | How many siblings do you have? (polite) |
| 언니 한 명하고 남동생 한 명 있어요. | Eonni han myeonghago namdongsaeng han myeong isseoyo. | I have one older sister and one younger brother. |
| 저는 외동이에요. | Jeoneun oedongieyo. | I'm an only child. |
| 저는 삼 남매 중에 막내예요. | Jeoneun sam nammae junge mangnaeyeyo. | I'm the youngest of three siblings. |
| 부모님은 부산에 사세요. | Bumonimeun Busane saseyo. | My parents live in Busan. |
| 우리 형은 회사원이에요. | Uri hyeongeun hoesawonieyo. | My older brother is an office worker. |

People are counted with native numbers and **명** (*myeong*), as you saw in [Korean numbers](/learn-korean/en/korean-numbers/): 한 명, 두 명, 세 명. **하고** links the two siblings, as in [how to say "and"](/learn-korean/en/korean-and/). Notice 사세요 for parents rather than 살아요: Koreans usually use the honorific -(으)세요 when talking about their own parents and grandparents.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A man calling his older brother 오빠 | 형 | 오빠 is only for female speakers. |
| A woman calling her older sister 누나 | 언니 | 누나 is only for male speakers. |
| 오빠 지민 | 지민 오빠 | The name comes first. |
| 엄마는 잘 지내세요? (about a friend's mom) | 어머님은 잘 지내세요? | Use the respectful form for others' parents. |
| 내 가족 | 우리 가족 | 우리 is the natural choice with family. |
| 이모 for your father's sister | 고모 | 이모 is your mother's sister. |
| 동생 오빠 | 남동생 | 오빠 means older, so a younger brother is 남동생. |

## FAQ

**Can a man say oppa?** Not to mean "older brother." 오빠 is used by female speakers; men use 형 for an older brother or older male friend. You may hear a man say 오빠 jokingly, but in normal speech it is a female word.

**Is oppa romantic?** Not by itself. It is the ordinary word a woman uses for her real older brother and for older male friends. Because women also call older boyfriends 오빠, the word can carry a sweet or flirty tone depending on context, which dramas love to play with.

**What do you call a younger sibling directly?** Usually by name. Korean has no special address term for younger siblings; 동생 is used to talk *about* them, not to call them.

**When can I start calling someone 언니 or 형?** When you are on friendly terms and the age gap is small. Koreans often check age early in a friendship partly for this reason. If you are unsure, use the person's name with 씨 (*ssi*), as in 수진 씨, until they suggest something closer.

**Why do Koreans say "our mom" instead of "my mom"?** Korean expresses belonging to a group with 우리 (*uri*), so family, home, school and country are usually "ours": 우리 엄마, 우리 집, 우리 학교. It does not mean the speaker shares the mother with the listener.

**What's next?** Practice describing family members with [question words](/learn-korean/en/korean-question-words/) such as 누구 (who) and 몇 (how many), and review [particles](/learn-korean/en/korean-particles/) so you can say things like 언니가 있어요 and 동생은 학생이에요 with confidence.
